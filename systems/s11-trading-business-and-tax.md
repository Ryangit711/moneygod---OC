# s11 — Trading Business and Tax (Canada)

### Personal Services Business (PSB) Risk
- If you incorporate but CRA decides you are providing services (trading) as if you were an employee (one client, directed how to work), your corp is a PSB.
- PSB income is taxed at ~33% federal + 12% BC = 45% — same as top personal rate. No SBD. No deduction flexibility.
- For a solo trader running your own capital through your own corp, this likely doesn't apply — you are not providing services TO someone. But if a prop firm dictates how/when you trade and you incorporate to run through them, CRA could argue PSB.
- Mitigation: own your own capital, use your own strategies, use your own software, set your own hours, trade your own account. If the prop firm is the source of 90%+ of your income and they control everything, you're an employee in disguise.

## Part 1: Sole Proprietorship vs. Corporation

### Income Tax — Sole Proprietor
- You report trading income (or loss) as business income on your T1 personal tax return via T2125 form (Statement of Business or Professional Activities).
- All net income is added to your marginal tax bracket. In BC (2025): bracket up to ~$47k = 20% fed+prov, ~$47k–$95k = 28%, ~$95k–$116k = 31%, ~$116k–$175k = 37%, ~$175k–$253k = 45%, over $253k = 53.5%.
- A sole proprietor pays CPP on the entire net income (self-employed CPP: 11.9% in 2025, both employer and employee portions). On ~$70k net, that's ~$8k in CPP alone.
- Losses flow through to your personal return — can offset other income (e.g., your 7-11 job wages). This matters early on.

### Income Tax — Incorporated
- Corporation pays federal + provincial general rate (~15% fed + 12% BC = ~27% on active business income up to $500k, subject to grind-down at ~$10M+ taxable capital).
- You control timing. Leave $ in the corp, pay the lower corporate rate (27%), let it compound inside the corp, then pay yourself dividends later (at personal rates) when you're in a lower bracket or via capital dividends.
- Small Business Deduction (SBD): ~$200k-$600k of active business income at ~12% federal (phased out based on taxable capital). BUT — is trading "active business"? CRA says no if you're a personal trading corp (more on this below). Likely 27% general rate for trading corps.
- Investment income inside a corp is NOT active business — it's subject to refundable taxes (Part IV tax on dividends, Part I on other investment income). It gets messy. A trading corp that actively trades might argue it's active business income (ACI) if it meets certain criteria. Do not assume this. Talk to an accountant who has dealt with trader corporations.

### When to Incorporate
- Net profits consistently above $100k+. Under that, the extra compliance cost ($1k-$3k/year for T2 return, bookkeeping, etc.) eats the tax savings.
- You want liability shielding (not much — you're not doing surgery. But if you ever manage other people's money, incorporate immediately.)
- You want income splitting with family (if they're shareholders, pay dividends. Budget 2024 hardened rules on this.)
- You want tax deferral: leave profits in the corp at ~27% instead of paying 45%+ personally.
- You want to access the Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption (LCGE) on sale of qualified small business corporation shares (QMBC shares). If your corp builds up investment/trading assets over time, it won't qualify. Structure matters.
- Bottom line: incorporate at $100k+ net consistent profit, but not a dollar sooner. The compliance overhead will bleed you.

### HST/GST Registration
- If you earn more than $30,000 in a single calendar quarter from your trading business (not net — gross revenue including gains + interest + dividends), you must register for GST/HST.
- "Wait, I buy and sell securities — that's financial services, exempt from GST/HST." Correct. BUT: your prop firm payouts, advisory fees, signal sales, education sales, or any non-security service — that is NOT exempt.
- If you charge ANYTHING that isn't "financial service" (e.g., coaching, subscriptions, signals, prop firm payouts structured as management fees), track the $30k threshold carefully.
- Once registered, you collect 5% GST (BC) or 5-15% HST (other provinces) on taxable supplies. You also get Input Tax Credits (ITCs) on expenses.
- If ALL your income is capital gains / dividends / interest from trading, you are not making taxable supplies and do NOT need to register. CRA guidance: trading your own account = no GST/HST obligation. Trading for others = registration required.

### Business Number / GST Number
- Business Number (BN): free, online at Canada.ca/Canada-Business-Network, 10 minutes. You get a 9-digit number.
- GST/HST number: done during the same application (add RT program account). No cost. Takes effect immediately.
- If you incorporate, the corp gets its own BN. You need one regardless.
- HST returns: filed annually if under $400k in taxable supplies, quarterly otherwise. Electronically via GST/HST NETFILE.

## Part 2: Capital Gains vs. Business Income

### The CRA Distinction: Trader vs. Investor
- Investor: buys/sells securities with reasonable expectation of long-term gain. Reports capital gains (50% inclusion rate). Can't deduct trading losses against employment income (only against capital gains).
- Trader (business income): buys/sells securities frequently, short holding periods, relies on price fluctuations for profit. Reports 100% of gains as business income. Can deduct losses against ANY income. No limit.
- The line is gray. CRA uses a series of factors from the *Smith v. The Queen* standard (1987) — you are a trader if:
  1. Frequency of transactions — many trades per month = trader
  2. Period of ownership — days/weeks = trader; years = investor
  3. Knowledge of securities markets — professional, trained, studied markets = trader
  4. Security purchases financed with debt (margin) = trader factor
  5. Trading is your primary source of income = trader
  6. You advertise, promote, or present as a trader = trader
  7. You maintain a formal trading business with records, business cards, office, separate accounts = trader
- No single factor decides it. But if you are actively day trading (holding minutes to hours, multiple trades per day, using leverage, studying charts, this is your main income source), CRA WILL rule you are in business — not investing.

### The Danger
- If CRA deems you a trader (business income), you pay 100% of gains as income — NOT the 50% capital gains inclusion.
- A $100k gain for an investor: $50k taxable income. For a trader: $100k taxable income. Difference at 45% bracket: $22.5k more tax.
- Prior years can be reassessed if CRA decides you were always a trader. Three-year reassessment window (four if you didn't report).
- CRA audits traders aggressively. They look at: trade frequency, holding periods, margin use, whether you have another source of income, whether you track trades, chat room use, social media (they scrape public profiles).
- You CANNOT elect to be treated as a capital gains taxpayer. It's determined by facts.

### How to Structure to Reduce Tax
- There is NO silver bullet to turn business income back into capital gains once CRA rules against you.
- But you can manage the risk:
  - If you hold positions longer than 12 months, argue investor intent. If you scalp, you are a trader. Period.
  - Keep a "long-term book" (hold > 1 year) and a "short-term book" (day trades). Two separate accounts. Argue the long-term book is capital account.
  - Incorporate — pay the ~27% corporate rate on trading income instead of personal marginal rates. Money stays inside, compounds at lower tax, withdrawn later.
  - Dividend strategy: once you have a corp, you can pay yourself capital dividends (tax-free) from the Capital Dividend Account (CDA) if you have capital gains inside the corp. BUT if your income is business income, the CDA does not apply. This only works if some portion is capital gains (e.g., your long-term hold book).
  - Use an RRSP or TFSA for the "investor" portfolio. Tax-free growth. The TFSA is especially powerful — gains are non-taxable and do not affect your tax return. BUT overtrading in a TFSA (pattern day trading) is considered carrying on a business inside TFSA — CRA has denied the tax exemption in multiple court cases. Keep TFSA buys to 1-3 trades per month max.

## Part 3: Deductions

### What You CAN Deduct (T2125 line items)
- **Home office**: Deduct a % of rent/mortgage interest (not principal), utilities, internet, insurance, property tax. Use square footage of dedicated trading space / total home square footage. Example: your desk corner is 100 sq ft of a 2,000 sq ft condo = 5%. If your rent is $2,000/month, claim $100/month ($1,200/year). Simplified method: CRA allows $5/sq ft up to 300 sq ft ($1,500 max) without detailed calculation.
- **Internet**: If you work from home and trade, deduct the business % of your internet bill. If you have 2 accounts (personal + business cell/internet), deduct 100%.
- **Computer & equipment**: Capital cost allowance (CCA) depreciation — generally 55% (Class 50 — computer hardware) declining balance. A $3,000 laptop = $1,650 CCA first year, ~$742 second year, etc.
- **Software subscriptions**: TradingView (pro/premium, $50/mo), data feeds (Level 2, $10-100/mo), prop firm evaluation fees ($50-500 per eval), broker platform fees (DAS, Sierra Chart, etc.), automated trading server costs (AWS, VPS).
- **Education**: Courses, books, webinars, mentoring — as long as it relates to improving your trading business. $5,000 on a trading course = full deduction. If CRA deems it "personal enrichment" vs. "business investment", they challenge it. Keep receipts and a one-paragraph note on how it relates to your business.
- **Margin interest**: Interest paid on margin loans is deductible IF it's for earning business income. T2125 line 8860. Keep statements showing interest charges.
- **Professional fees**: Accountant, lawyer, bookkeeper for trade-related work.
- **Bank fees**: Separate business account fees, wire fees, foreign exchange fees.
- **Office supplies**: Pens, paper, monitor stand, chair (if >$1,000, CCA it — it's Class 8, 20% declining balance).

### What You CANNOT Deduct
- **Meals**: Unless you meet with a client or professional (accountant, broker) AND discuss business. A meal at your desk while trading is NOT deductible.
- **Entertainment**: No. Even if you review charts at a bar.
- **Auto expenses**: If you trade from home (like you do), your car has nothing to do with trading. Zero deduction.
- **Clothing**: No matter how much you look like a trader.
- **Personal trading account losses**: If you have a separate personal account, losses there are not deductible against business income.
- **TFSA/RRSP losses**: Not deductible. Ever. By law.
- **Fines**: CRA penalties, interest on late filing — not deductible.

### Vehicle and Travel
- If you trade from home full time, no vehicle deduction.
- If you travel to a trader meetup, conference, or to meet a client/professional face-to-face, the travel portion (flights, hotels, meals at the conference) is deductible. Keep agenda/schedule proving business purpose.
- T1 line 9281 (travel expenses) and T1 line 9282 (meals and entertainment — 50% deductible).
- Cost of attending a trading conference in Vegas: flight ($400), hotel ($800, 3 nights), conference fee ($1,500), meals during conference ($150 after 50% haircut = $75). Total ~$2,775 deduction.

### Track Everything in a Separate Business Account
- Open a separate bank account (Tangerine, Simplii, or free online bank) used ONLY for trading business income and expenses.
- Mix personal and business transactions = CRA will manually audit every line. You lose credibility and deduction arguments.
- Same for a separate credit card (USD and CAD) used only for trading expenses.
- Bookkeeping requirement: CRA expects you to keep records for 6 years. T2125, receipts, trade confirmations, broker statements, bank statements. Digital copies are fine.

## Part 4: USD Accounts and FX Issues

### Trading in USD, Reporting in CAD
- All amounts on your T1 return are in CAD.
- Every trade, every gain/loss, every expense in USD must be converted to CAD at the Bank of Canada daily exchange rate (or reasonable average rate).
- The CRA requires "reasonable" exchange rate method. Simplest: use Bank of Canada annual average rate for all transactions in a year. For 2025, the annual average BC rate was ~1.36-1.37 CAD/USD. More precision is better if you're doing high-volume trading.
- You do NOT convert gains/losses per trade individually unless CRA requests it. Use the annual average rate for simplicity. But keep records in case they do.

### Foreign Exchange Gains — Separate and Reportable
- Foreign exchange gains on the principal amount of USD itself are a separate item.
- Example: You deposit $100k USD when CAD/USD = 1.30 ($130k CAD). You withdraw $100k USD when CAD/USD = 1.40 ($140k CAD). You have a $10k CAD FX gain — reportable as business income (or capital gain).
- FX gains on the cash USD balance are real. CRA expects them reported.
- Keep a running CAD-adjusted ledger of your USD cash balance and calculate FX P&L quarterly.

### USD Bank Account
- Open one. TD, RBC, BMO, and HSBC offer true USD accounts (TD USD Daily Interest, RBC USD High Interest eSavings, etc.).
- Or use Wise (formerly TransferWise) — multi-currency account, cheap conversion, good for receiving USD from brokers, prop firms.
- Interactive Brokers (IBKR) has a built-in USD account — you can deposit and hold USD without conversion.
- PayPal USD is fine for small amounts, but holds >$10k USD are risky (they freeze accounts).

### USD Credit Card
- Get one for USD-denominated expenses (TradingView, data feeds, AWS servers in USD, prop firm eval fees).
- Charges are processed in USD — you avoid the 2.5% foreign transaction fee that CAD cards charge.
- BMO USD Mastercard, Rogers World Elite Mastercard (4% cashback on USD — nets the FX fee), or Home Trust Preferred Visa.
- Business version: BMO USD Business Mastercard. Pair it with your trading corp or sole prop.

### Norbert's Gambit
- Cheap way to convert CAD↔USD without paying bank spreads (banks charge 2-3% on FX).
- Buy DLR.TO (Canadian-listed ETF that tracks the US dollar) in CAD, journal it (transfer) to your USD account as DLR.U.TO, sell the USD shares. Net cost: one commission ($0-10 at IBKR, free at Wealthsimple? no, Wealthsimple charges) and the bid-ask spread (~0.1%).
- After journaling, you have USD in your brokerage account at near-spot rate.
- Works best with a broker that supports dual-listed securities and journaling: Interactive Brokers (best), RBC Direct Investing, BMO InvestorLine, TD Direct Investing.
- At IBKR, FX conversion is already cheap (~0.002% on $10k+). Norbert's Gambit only matters with other banks.

## Part 5: Prop Firms and Taxes

### The Gray Area
- Prop firms (FTMO, The Funded Trader, Earn2Trade, TopStep, MyForexFunds before collapse, etc.) typically give you a "funded account" — you trade firm capital, split profits 70/30 to 90/10.
- CRA has no specific guidance on prop firm income for Canadian traders. Here's what we know:
  - If the firm treats you as a contractor (you sign a service agreement), the payout is business income — reportable on T2125.
  - If the firm treats you as an employee (almost never for prop firms), it's employment income — T4.
  - If the payout is structured as a "gift," "bonus," or "prize," CRA doesn't care about your firm's label. Substance over form: if you earned it by trading, it's income from a business.
  - Some prop firms issue 1099-NEC (for US residents) — this is NOT a Canadian tax form. Ignore the US categorization. You report it properly in Canada per CRA rules.
- There is a risk that CRA considers prop firm payouts as "foreign business income" if the firm is based outside Canada. This has no special tax treatment — it's just business income.

### Evaluation Fees
- Fully deductible as a business expense. The cost to take a challenge ($50-$1,000) is an ordinary expense of generating trading income.
- If you fail multiple challenges, all fees are deductible.
- Keep receipts. Prop firms often vanish or change platforms. Have PDFs.

### Payouts
- Report as gross business income (include on T2125 as "Gross income from business").
- Deduct all associated expenses (evaluation fees, platform fees, server costs, data subscriptions, any management fee the firm charges).
- If the firm withholds any tax (some US-based firms withhold 15-30% for non-resident aliens under FATCA), you file Form T2209 (Foreign Tax Credits) to get a credit against your Canadian tax for the withheld amount.
- Most prop firms DO NOT withhold tax for Canadians because there's no US payroll. But double-check. If they withhold, get a receipt.

### CRA Audit Triggers for Traders
- Filing a T2125 with large income and low expenses — looks like you're hiding something.
- Claiming a home office deduction when you also claim auto expenses — auto wins every time (CRA sees it as impossible for both to be legitimate unless you're a plumber).
- High ratio of business losses to income — CRA flags 3+ consecutive years of losses. They will argue it's a hobby, not a business. Mitigation: show profit in at least 3 of 5 years (CRA's "reasonable expectation of profit" test — softened since *Stewart* 2002 but still a factor).
- Large USD cash deposits into personal accounts without clear sourcing — CRA asks: "Where did this come from?" Have broker statements ready.
- Gross revenue over $200k — higher scrutiny tier.
- Using the same account for personal and business transactions.
- Reporting capital gains when your trade frequency screams "business income." CRA has automated matching tools that flag accounts with >50 trades/year on the TSX or >200 trades/year on US exchanges. They WILL reassess.
- If flagged, you get a letter requesting: trade confirmations, broker statements, bank statements, trading logs, expense receipts, business plan, your rationale for trader vs. investor classification. You have 30 days to respond. Extensions are possible but look evasive.
- An audit can cover 3-4 prior tax years. If CRA determines you are a trader (not investor) for one year, they will reassess all open years.

### Records to Keep (Per Prop Firm)
- Signed agreement (contract)
- Evaluation fee payment receipts
- Payout receipts (date, amount USD/CAD, method)
- Firm's tax forms (if any)
- Correspondence about your trader status

### Year-End Tax Planning (November-December)
- By Nov 1, estimate your full-year trading P&L and compare to your 7-11 employment income.
- Defer income: if you're a sole prop, you can choose your fiscal year end (Dec 31 matches calendar for most). You cannot defer past Dec 31 easily — but you can delay billing/withdrawals for incorporated accounts.
- Realize losses: if you have realized gains, sell losing positions before Dec 31 to trigger losses that offset gains (business loss if you're a trader, capital loss if investor). Wash-up rule for superficial losses applies if you repurchase within 30 days — the loss is denied.
- RRSP contribution: You can contribute until March 1 of the next year for the prior year deduction. If you made $80k trading income + $30k from job = $110k, your RRSP room is 18% of $110k = $19.8k (minus pension adjustment). Contribute as much as possible, deduct against top bracket. Refund: at 37% bracket, $19.8k RRSP = ~$7.3k refund.
- FHSA (First Home Savings Account): If you are a first-time home buyer, contribute up to $8k/year ($40k lifetime). Same tax deduction as RRSP, withdrawals (for qualifying home purchase) are tax-free. Max tax benefit at 37% bracket: $2,960/year.
- TFSA contribution: $7,000/year (2025 limit, indexed). Tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawal. No deduction on deposit but gains never taxed. Use for long-term holdings outside your trading business account.
- Installments: If your tax owing in a year exceeds $3,000 (federal) and $3,000 (BC provincial), CRA requires quarterly installments for the next year (Mar 15, Jun 15, Sep 15, Dec 15). Miss them and you pay interest (CRA's prescribed rate, currently 8% — painful). Automate this.

### Alternative Minimum Tax (AMT) — 2024 Reforms
- Effective 2024, AMT rules tightened. AMT rate increased from 15% to 20.5%. Exemption raised to ~$173k (indexed) for AMT calculation.
- AMT applies if you have large capital gains deductions, stock option deductions, or other preference items.
- For traders reporting business income (not capital gains), AMT impact is minimal — business income is not a preference item.
- If you have a mix of capital gains (long-term book) and business income (day trading), and use the capital gains inclusion reduction (50% vs. 100%) or use capital gains deduction (LCGE), AMT may trigger. Don't assume you're safe — have your accountant run an AMT projection.
- AMT paid can be carried forward 7 years and credited against regular tax in those years. Not lost, just deferred.

## Part 6: The Practical Steps (Today)

### 1. Open a Separate Bank Account for Trading
- Any no-fee online bank (Tangerine, Simplii, PC Financial, Motive Financial)
- Do NOT commingle. One account for trading income, trading expenses, and nothing else.
- Also open a separate USD account (wise.com, or a personal USD account at any Big 5 bank).
- Link it to your brokerage account for fast withdrawals.

### 2. Get a Business Number
- Go to canada.ca/business-number.html
- Answer: sole proprietorship, trading financial instruments.
- Takes 10 minutes. Free.
- Write down your 9-digit BN (e.g., 123456789 RT0001).
- You can add a GST/HST program account later if needed.

### 3. Track All Expenses from Day One
- Spreadsheet (Google Sheets) or accounting software (Wave — free, QuickBooks Simple Start — ~$15/mo).
- Columns: Date, Category, Description, Amount (CAD), Receipt link, Business purpose.
- Enter every subscription, broker fee, eval fee, internet bill proportion, office supply, computer.
- Track USD expenses separately. Convert to CAD at date of transaction using Bank of Canada rate. Keep a running total.

### 4. Understand Broker Tax Forms
- **IBKR (Interactive Brokers)**: Provides Activity Statement (monthly and annual) with realized P&L in USD/CAD, FX conversion, dividend summaries, and margin interest. The "Flex Query" system lets you build custom reports. Use it to generate year-to-date P&L per instrument. Export CSV.
- **Questrade**: Provides a "Trade Confirmation Report" downloadable by date range. Also an "Account Statement" monthly. Questrade does NOT automatically convert to CAD for tax purposes — you must do your own conversion. They provide CAD equivalent per trade, but check the rate — it's the trade date rate, not the settlement date rate. CRA accepts either as long as consistent.
- **Wealthsimple Trade**: Provides "Tax Documents" (T5008 for accounts under certain thresholds, but the T5008 only shows proceeds, not cost basis, for Canadian securities). For USD trading, WS uses a terrible FX rate. Do not rely on their numbers. Export trade history and calculate yourself.
- **TD Direct Investing / RBC Direct / BMO InvestorLine**: Provide annual T5008 (Statement of Securities Transactions) which reports proceeds of disposition. This is for capital gains reporting — inadequate for business income reporting. If you are a trader, do NOT use the T5008 as your tax return information. It is incomplete. Use your own trade log.
- **T5008 filing**: The broker sends a copy to CRA. If your trade activity creates a T5008 that reports capital gains but you file as business income, CRA's automated matching may flag a discrepancy. Pre-emptive step: file a letter with your return explaining: "All securities transactions reported on T5008 are business income, not capital gains. Full details on T2125 attached." Your accountant should handle this.
- **Dividends**: T5 or T3 slips for Canadian dividends. For US dividends (paid by US stocks), you get a 1042-S (US non-resident withholding) and must convert to CAD. The withholding tax (15% of US dividends for Canadian residents) is creditable against Canadian tax via T2209.

### 5. Talk to an Accountant at Month 3 (Consistent Profitability)
- Find a CPA who works with traders. Not a general household tax filer — a business/trading specialist.
- Interview questions: "How many trader clients do you have? Do you know the capital gains vs. business income factors? Have you dealt with prop firm income? Do you file T2125 and T2 returns?"
- Budget: $500-1,500 for a sole prop year-end filing, $1,500-3,000 for a T2 corporate return.
- DO NOT use TurboTax or UFile for a trading business. Hire a human.

### 5. File T2125 Correctly
- T2125 (Statement of Business or Professional Activities) is the form you file with your T1 return if you are a sole proprietor trader.
- Gross income: Line 8299 (business income). ALL trading gains, dividends, interest earned from the trading business. Not just net — gross. CRA wants to see total business revenue.
- COGS (Cost of Goods Sold): For traders, this is your cost basis on securities sold. Line 8320: "Purchases, including subcontracts." List the total cost of all securities purchased during the year. Line 8325: "Subtotal (cost of goods sold)" — your net cost after adjustments.
- Gross profit: Line 8299 minus cost of goods sold.
- Deductions: Lines 9270-9946 for various expense categories.
- Net income (loss): Line 9369. This flows to your T1 return as business income.
- If you use an online brokerage (IBKR, Questrade, Wealthsimple), request the annual tax package. It summarizes proceeds, cost bases, and FX. But you still need to categorize properly — the broker report is not a T2125.
- IBKR provides a "Tax Optimization" report and "Activity Statement" broken down by instrument type. Questrade provides a CAD-equivalent trade summary. Do not blindly offshore — understand each line.

### 6. Keep a Ledger: Date, Amount, Description, CAD Equivalent
- For every inflow: source, date, gross amount (before fees), fees deducted, net amount, exchange rate at date, CAD equivalent.
- For every outflow: same plus business purpose.
- This one file ($profits_ledger_YYYY.csv) will save your ass in an audit.
- Format:

### Responding to a CRA Audit
- Do not panic. An audit is a document review, not a criminal investigation.
- Do not represent yourself. Hire a CPA who does tax litigation support. Expect $2k-$5k for representation.
- Do not give the auditor anything beyond what they request. Keep responses specific and narrow.
- Policies to have ready: written trading plan, trade log with timestamps, business registration documents, books and records for all years under audit, all broker statements, all bank statements.
- If the auditor proposes reassessing you from investor to trader status, present your written case: holding periods, intent, frequency, risk management, business structure. Case law: *Smith*, *Fries*, *Tonks* — argue the facts that support your position.
- You have the right to appeal a reassessment to the Tax Court of Canada (informal procedure for amounts under $25k). Most auditor decisions are overturned or reduced on appeal if you have documentation. No documentation = no case.
- Audit survival is 80% documentation, 20% representation. Start now.

### Superficial Loss Rules — Trader vs. Investor
- Superficial loss: If you sell a security at a loss and you (or an affiliated person) buys the same or identical property within 30 days before or after the sale, the loss is denied.
- For investors: The denied loss is added to the ACB (adjusted cost base) of the repurchased shares. Deferred, not lost.
- For traders (business income): The denied loss is NOT added to cost base — it is simply denied. CRA policy for traders is stricter because the superficial loss rules were designed for capital property.
- This means: if you are a trader and you scalp the same stock repeatedly within 30 days, some losses may be denied.
- Mitigation: Trade different expiry dates, different instruments, or hold >30 days between re-entry. For high-frequency traders, swap to a correlated but not identical name for 30 days. Not perfect — CRA looks at "identical property." For HFT in a single stock, the rule bites you. Work with an accountant on a strategy or accept the denied losses as a cost of business.
